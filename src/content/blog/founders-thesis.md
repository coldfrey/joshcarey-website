For the last 20 years software was a moat. You needed people who knew how to build and ship a product, find the customers, and do it faster than the next team. It was pretty rare, and worth backing when you found it. AI has made technical competence cheap. An MVP or a first draft is now an afternoon's work. The most reliable bet in venture was always the founder who had done it before. However how do you decern the first timers?

You would think that if machines do the work, the person matters less. In fact, its the oposite. Leverage magnifies whoever holds it. Hand a mediocore founder powerful tools and you get more mediocrity, faster. Hand them to a great one and the distance between the two is massive. So the gap between strong founders and an average one is not closing its widening. One of the core ideas that no tool can replace is ownership. The best founders carry their companies, they can't put it down and they can't deligate caring about it. They don't leave when it gets tough because it's theirs, the entity they fostered and built in a way the CAP table can never truely convey. AI can't replace this, or generate this. You cannot hire this ownership. You can only discover it. This is the game, one of discovery but not of ideas, or market, or decks. Of people.

We are not short on theories about what makes a great founder. The research highlights a cluster: openness to novelty and adventure, curiosity, conscientiousness, emotional intelligence, calculated risk management, stress tolerance. More recent thinking ranks taste, the judgment to know what is worth building when anything can be built, and a bias to act. None of this is secret. It's well documented. Yet when you examine how founders are actually selected, the story is different. A warm intro, a polished pitch, a rehearsed story. These reward the salesman who performs well in the meeting, which is not the same person who will carry a company for ten years. That's the gap. We know what matters. We just don't have a good way to measure it.

So how do we see what the meeting hides? How do we strip back the fabric and expose the raw traits of the person underneath? You stop letting them perform. A pitch is rehearsed and built to hit the right notes. Take someone out of their comfort zone and into real conditions. Adversity doesn't build character. It reveals it.

What demonstrates character clearly?

Adventure demonstrates character on a level you'll never see in a meeting. You see whether someone steps up and leads. How they learn from those with more experience. How curious they are. Their approach to risk. Their humility. Do they freeze or do they thrive when the scenario is unstable, unknown, unpredictable? These responses can't be faked, because they have to be backed with physical action, and they can't be rehearsed, because the scenario is unpredictable.
